## Reviewer 1

**Recommendation:** WEAK REJECT (2)

### Summary and High Level Discussion

The paper proposes a generic Resource Allocation via Speculative Preview (RASP) framework that uses predictive signals from a draft model to guide token-level allocation before target-model verification to perform fine-grained compute resource adjustment. They showed an example of RASP for MoE routing using Spec-K that uses speculative decoding-based preview to estimate token uncertainty and reallocates the Top-k budget dynamically. Experiments on distributed MoE inference showed speedup over fixed top-k allocation.

## Reviewer 2

**Recommendation:** WEAK ACCEPT (3)

### Summary and High Level Discussion

The paper Spec-K, discussed the inefficient static resource allocation in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) large language models (LLMs) during inference, especially under speculative decoding and long contextual sequences. Spec-K proposed RASP (Resource Allocation via Speculative Preview), a framework for adaptive LLM inference. This training-free method dynamically adjusts MoE Top-k routing budgets based on token-uncertainty estimates from a draft model. The paper provides an analysis of the method, an efficient optimization strategy for policy selection, and experiments across multiple models and benchmarks. Results show throughput improvements with only negligible accuracy loss.

### Strengths

+ The RASP framework is well-organized into 3 steps: estimating how hard each token is, deciding how many experts to use, and executing accordingly. + The work is motivated by practical inefficiencies in modern serving, particularly as highlighted in the Introduction and Background (Pages 1-3). + The latency breakdowns (Figures 4a/b) show the speedups come from reduced computation and communication, and these line up closely with what the allocation plan predicts. Lower routing budgets also improve draft-target acceptance rates (Figure 5), which was not expected and adds an extra layer of practical value. + The experiments span a wide range of models: Qwen3, DeepSeek, and GLM to 671B parameters. The throughput gains hold consistently, and the results in Figures 3-6 and Tables I/II break down the results at both the system and component levels. ### Weaknesses

- Comparison with baseline could be improved. The paper skips comparisons with several recent methods that tackle the same problem speculative MoE inference and dynamic expert allocation. - A number of recent works have explored similar territory: utility-weighted speculation, dynamic Top-k, and expert budgeting. There are other signals available from the draft model (attention patterns, cross-layer activations, token context that could potentially do better in cases where perplexity alone is not a reliable indicator of token difficulty. ### Comments for Authors

The system results and the technical work are good. However, the paper should directly compare with the most relevant recent methods for speculative MoE inference and dynamic Top-k routing. The authors also cite some recent works, which could have served as a baseline as well. "LYNX: Enabling Efficient MoE Inference Through Dynamic Batch-Aware Expert Selection" was cited as Lynx [54] (Gupta et al., 2024) in the related works. ## Reviewer 3

**Recommendation:** ACCEPT (4)

### Summary and High Level Discussion

This work improves the execution time of MoE models by using the speculative preview stage of token analysis to allocate Top-k budget to each token proportionally to the predicted uncertainty. In particular for current MoE models, since both computation and communication scales with the uncertainty, this leads to a neat improvement in execution time with very limited extra work/coding, and it can use data the model already must compute.

### Strengths

First, the core idea of the paper is a simple, but elegant, insight. We already pay for a draft model, so using the information from it more efficiently is a clear advance - and much simpler than extra models/classifiers/retraining/additional passes. I also find it a key strength that the mechanism is very lightweight. In theory it is also an interesting approach that this can generalize to using preview signals to allocation policy and the adaptive execution. Third, I also appreciate that the paper is evaluated on cutting-edge actual models relevant for HPC scale. The results are useful for real-world applications, and they show they can reduce Top-k quite substantially before quality starts to degrade.

### Weaknesses

One weakness is that the whole approach relies on draft model perplexity being a good predictor of target-model difficulty, and in particular the *computational* and *communication* load. The authors make a good case of showing this heuristically for MoE with Top-k execution, but the paper would improve with a stronger theoretical analysis of this correlation. Second, I think it's a much more difficult question to assess whether this is generally true in terms of the "framework" suggested. The authors do not show data for any other application, and there are several examples where such variances might not be linear or smooth. My guess it that a general framework is considerably more complicated than the special case presented, and this part reads more as a stretch. I am also not convinced how important they Bayesian optimization is. Finally, the observation that alignment between draft and final model increases is interesting. Now, one way of interpreting this (as the authors do) is that it's a strength, but an alternative possibility is that traditional (constant) top-k allocation causes the model to better explore paths where the draft model was incorrect. If we reduce those paths based on the signal in the draft model we will definitely improve the alignment - but that comes at the cost of quality. Other questions is what would happen when the draft model is small/poor, or if speculative acceptance is quite low? ## Reviewer 5

**Recommendation:** ACCEPT (4)

### Summary and High Level Discussion

The paper "Spec-K: Adaptive Resource Allocation via Speculative Preview for Efficient MoE Inference" introduces an optimization to MoE LLM inference. The approach assumes that speculative decoding is being used - small models predicts tokens at fast pace, then larger model validates them in more efficient batched fashion. If predictions do not match - sequence gets recomputed from that place. The key idea is to use perplexity from that small model to tell which tokens are "easy", and route those token top less experts than model was designed to do. That allows to save no only con compute, but on network traffic as well.

### Strengths

Real speedup - 1.72x real-world throughput speedup, optimilty on Paretto frontier.

### Weaknesses

The routing depends on draft model - if draft model is erroneously confinmdent - that will route tokens to less experts, and that in tern might prevent large model from correcting. While small performance drop - I would expect it to happen exactly on edge cases, which is not necessarily desirable.

### Comments for Rebuttal

Have you encountered systemic edge cases in niche domains like code generation or non-English languages, where the draft model is highly confident but entirely wrong, tricking the target model into an artificially low routing budget and causing a cascade of rejected tokens? Why compare against a completely flat Top-8 or Top-4 baseline for everything? It is believed that lower layers of the model are doing simpler processing - reducing experts just in the lower layers could have been a better baseline.

## Authors’ Rebuttal of Reviews

We thank the reviewers for the constructive comments. Several comments overlap, so we first clarify four points that are already present in the submission but may not have been explicit enough: dynamic Top-k baselines, the preview signal, robustness under draft-target mismatch, and system attribution/load balance. We then answer reviewer-specific details. ## Common 1 (R2/R5): Dynamic Top-k Baselines and Available Redundancy Several comments ask why related dynamic expert-allocation methods were not used as baselines, and how Spec-K should be interpreted beyond a Top-k heuristic. The method has two necessary conditions: **the preview signal must be predictive enough, and the target computation must contain reducible redundancy.** Common 1 addresses the second condition; Common 2 addresses the first. The submitted evaluation emphasizes absolute quality-efficiency frontiers because RASP/Spec-K's speedup is bounded by the available routing redundancy in a given model/task/system. **RASP/Spec-K does not create speedup out of thin air; it removes redundant execution when the model/system already has it**. Sec. V-C scopes this effect: DeepSeek-R1 has a broad lossless region, while GLM-4.7-Flash with base Top-k 4 has much less room. The Pareto frontiers also show that Spec-K locates lossless regions and the optimum borderline across architectures/tasks. Among the related dynamic allocation methods, the most directly comparable result currently available is Lynx. On Qwen3-30B-A3B with GSM8K, Lynx reports about 1.14x speedup with a 0.8% accuracy drop, while Spec-K reaches substantially larger throughput gains at negligible loss in the same model family. We did not include Lynx in the original paper because a fair comparison was non-trivial before submission: Lynx was not open-source and used different serving-stack/model-task setups. Only after their May 2026 revision did the corresponding datapoint become available for an equivalent comparison. We will include this comparison in the revision. ## Common 2 (R2/R5): Preview Signal Effectiveness To scope the first condition above, we compared Spec-K with the lower-layer fixed-k baseline suggested by R5. This baseline ignores draft perplexity and simply reduces the first `n` MoE layers to a constant k while preserving base Top-k elsewhere. It is dominated by Spec-K on Qwen3-30B-A3B+Qwen3-0.6B, GSM8K-CoT. A representative fixed lower-layer policy reaches `(Top-k=6.01/8,score=88.93%)`. At essentially the same budget, Spec-K reaches `(Top-k=5.96/8,score=91.13%)`, a +2.2 point gain. At essentially the same score, Spec-K reaches `(Top-k=5.14/8,score=88.40%)`, saving about 14.5% of the routing budget relative to the fixed-layer baseline. This directly answers whether the preview signal is doing useful work: unconditional layer reduction can save work, but it does not find the same quality-efficiency frontier. ## Common 3 (R2/R3/R5): Draft-Target Alignment and Edge Cases R3/R5 ask what happens when the draft model is confidently wrong. This is the robustness side of the preview mechanism in Common 2. Spec-K inherits the same prerequisite as practical speculative decoding: the draft-target pair must be highly aligned. This is not limited to one draft type. Sec. V covers a pretrained standalone draft model, hidden-state-based EAGLE, and native MTP; all are designed to align with the target model's internal representations, and RASP builds on that alignment rather than introducing an extra predictor. Allocation is also less brittle than a binary accept/reject decision because the target execution has a plateau region, as shown in Sec. V-C. If the draft fails to capture the generation direction, Sec. III-E's sequence-level self-regulation provides a second guard: higher subsequent uncertainty increases later Top-k, preventing an aggressive decision from accumulating into a systematic failure. The IFEval result in Table II illustrates the boundary: the conservative Lossless policy remains stable, while a reasoning-calibrated Optimum policy can be too aggressive for the non-thinking/instruction-following phase; phase-aware policy selection or separate offline calibration can address this without fine-tuning or online search. ## Common 4 (R1/R4): System Attribution and Load Balance R1/R4 ask for stronger attribution of the speedup. Sec. III-B states the direct system effect: lower average Top-k reduces token-expert assignments, which reduces GroupGEMM work and All-to-All payload. Sec. IV-B describes the adaptive kernel support, and Fig. 4 provides the latency attribution. The observed gains follow the expected performance model of adaptive GroupGEMM and All-to-All kernels: reducing dynamic MoE workload reduces compute and communication nearly proportionally. We will add an HPC-oriented kernel/workload analysis that varies Spec-K-induced workload to make this model explicit. For load balance, we distinguish relative skew from absolute critical-path assignment volume, matching the submitted discussion of Combine/synchronization latency in Fig. 4. On a GSM8K runtime sweep, Lossless and Optimum reduce the EP32 critical-path device assignment volume to 0.68x and 0.59x of baseline, respectively. Relative skew metrics can rise modestly because total work falls faster than the lightest buckets, but the absolute synchronization-relevant work decreases. This is consistent with Fig. 4, where Combine/synchronization latency also decreases. ## Reviewer-Specific Notes R1: Common 4 addresses the requested attribution/load-balance analysis. The BoTorch optimization is offline calibration only. Runtime uses the selected piecewise policy and simple masking; there is no online Bayesian optimization, no per-batch search, and no extra model. We will also clarify symbol definitions, the Top-k cap, realistic-serving setup, and code-release plan. The policy is capped by the model's trained base Top-k because Spec-K removes low-ranked experts; it does not invent additional expert capacity beyond the model's original routing. R2: Common 1 addresses related dynamic Top-k baselines and the Lynx comparison; Common 2 addresses the draft-perplexity signal; Common 3 addresses phase-aware policies. RASP is not a generalization of prior dynamic Top-k methods; it is a new preview-guided resource-allocation framework. R3/R5: Common 1 addresses baseline rationale; Common 2 addresses whether draft perplexity is informative and whether lower-layer reduction is a competitive alternative; Common 3 addresses the confident-but-wrong edge case. Poor draft-target alignment is a real limitation, but modern speculative deployments already require high alignment for acceptance.
